Cooling by Location

How do different data center cooling methods trade local water against carbon emissions, and which method fits where? We mapped every state, then tested our pick in three very different hubs.

Research question

Is direct-to-chip liquid cooling the best choice everywhere, or does the right cooling method depend on local water and the local power grid?

Summary

Key findings

What the data shows across all 50 states, DC and our three case-study hubs.

  1. 27% Data centers are highly concentrated.

    Virginia and Texas hold 984 of the 3,712 data centers counted, about 27% of the U.S. total.8

  2. 1,307MW The fastest growth is in the driest hub.

    Phoenix has 1,307 MW under construction, more than the 904 MW it runs today, with just 7.2 inches of rain a year, in a state rated High for water stress.1,5,15

  3. TX > VA The grid matters as much as water.

    Weighted by grid carbon, Texas outranks Virginia despite having fewer data centers.9

  4. 3of 3 hubs Recommendation: direct-to-chip liquid cooling.

    It is the only method rated a good fit in all three hubs, but each location needs different support, from reclaimed water to clean power.

05 · Recommendation

Direct-to-chip liquid cooling, by default.

A sealed loop carries heat straight off the chip and reuses the same water, losing only 0–5% of it. What it needs alongside it depends on the location.

A

Appendix

Methods and limitations

Data and definitions

  • Data centers Facility counts by state from one tracker (Electric Choice, October 2026). Other trackers report different totals, so counts are best read as relative.
  • Grid carbon EPA eGRID 2023 CO₂ output emission rate, in pounds per megawatt-hour, for each state.
  • Water stress WRI Aqueduct 4.0 baseline water stress: the share of available surface and groundwater that is withdrawn each year, scored 0 (low) to 5 (extremely high). WRI scores river basins; each state's score is the area-weighted average of the basins inside it, using the U.S. extract shared by the independent analysis.
  • Rainfall NOAA state average annual precipitation, 1971–2000.
  • Hubs Metro-level figures. Capacity from JLL (mid-2025), rainfall at the main airport station (1991–2020), and carbon for the regional grid that serves the metro, so hub and state numbers can differ.
  • Map colors Water stress uses WRI's five categories (Low through Extremely High). The other measures group states into seven equal-count bands.

Limitations

  • Water stress is a state average. A single basin can be far more stressed than its state's score, and a large, lightly used area can pull a state's average down. Hawaii is not covered.
  • Hub numbers mix levels. Hub capacity, rainfall and grid carbon are metro-level, but water stress is the statewide average.
  • Cooling fit ratings are qualitative. They are the team's judgment based on the sources, not measured results.
  • Company disclosure is limited. Most operators do not report which cooling method each site uses.
