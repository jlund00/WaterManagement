What it really costs to keep data centers cool. We compared four cooling methods on local water and carbon emissions, mapped every state, and tested them in three very different hubs.

Research question

Which cooling method best balances water and carbon? Does the answer change by location? And how can AI help?

Why it matters

A data center's water footprint is bigger than it looks.

With the AI boom, more and bigger data centers are being built than ever, and cooling is one of the biggest drivers of their water and energy use.

~6,500homes' worth of water

A typical 100 MW data center in the U.S. uses about 2 million liters a day, including water at the power plants that supply it.20

~2×by 2030

Global data center water use could grow from about 560 to 1,200 billion liters a year.20

2/3used at power plants

Most of a data center's water footprint comes from the electricity it uses, so every extra unit of power costs water too.20

1. Operational water-energy equations

Following Li et al. (2023), "Making AI Less 'Thirsty'"16, total operational water is on-site cooling evaporation (Scope 1) plus off-site power generation water (Scope 2):

E_total  = IT_Load_kW × 8,760 hrs × PUE
W_Scope1 = IT_Energy_kWh × WUE_onsite
W_Scope2 = E_total_kWh × EWIF_grid
W_Total  = W_Scope1 + W_Scope2

2. Thermal density & climate bounds

ASHRAE TC 9.9 rack thresholds17: air-cooled CRAC / CRAH systems hit heat-rejection ceilings around 40–50 kW/rack. AI clusters at 100–200 kW/rack need direct-to-chip cold plates or immersion.

Climate PUE & WUE modifiers: hot, dry climates maximize evaporative efficiency (low PUE) at the cost of high evaporation (high WUE). Cold climates allow air-side and water-side economizers.

NREL EWIF18: power plant water ranges from about 0.5 L/kWh (renewable or dry-cooled) to 1.8 L/kWh (U.S. average) and above 4.5 L/kWh (once-through thermoelectric and high-evaporation hydro).

3. Site-specific parameters not assumed

An investment-grade study would also need: (a) cooling tower cycles of concentration and blowdown / sewer fees; (b) IT heat-capture ratio (typically 70–85% captured by cold plates, the rest by rear-door heat exchangers or trim air cooling); (c) demand charges ($/kW-month) versus volumetric tariffs; and (d) PFAS / dielectric fluid permitting for two-phase immersion.

Method PUE and WUE come from the team's calculator prototype. Climate adjustments, the fit score and switching costs are the team's simplified assumptions (see Methods).

04 · Recommendation

Direct-to-chip liquid cooling, by default.

A sealed loop carries heat straight off the chip and reuses the same water, losing only 0–5% of it. What it needs alongside it depends on the location.

Where AI helps

  • Tunes fans, pumps and temperature setpoints in real time, so cooling uses only the energy it needs. Google's DeepMind cut the energy used to cool its data centers by up to 40%.19
  • Predicts heat loads and equipment failures before they happen.
  • Shifts flexible computing to cooler hours, or to times when the grid is cleaner.

Where it falls short

  • It can't fix the wrong cooling method: a perfectly tuned evaporative tower in Phoenix still uses Phoenix's water.
  • It can't offset its own growth: AI is why so many new data centers are being built in the first place.
  • It only optimizes what operators measure, and most don't report their cooling water.

How we'd put AI to work

Inside the data center

Cooling AI runs on the site's own servers. It reacts in seconds, keeps working if the internet connection drops, keeps facility data private, and uses only a tiny fraction of the site's power, so its savings far outweigh its cost.

Before anything is built

Outside data and forecasts (drought and water stress from WRI and NOAA, grid carbon outlooks from utilities) help choose a location where the cooling method will hold up for decades.

Future research

  1. Score individual sites with basin-level water stress instead of state averages.
  2. Measure the energy cost of closed-loop and zero-water cooling designs.
  3. Test the guide against real operator data as more companies report their cooling water use.
  4. Examine where AI-driven cooling optimization helps most, and its limits.
Methods, limitations & sourcesHow the numbers were built, what they leave out, and where they come from

Data and definitions

  • Data centers Facility counts by state from one tracker (Electric Choice, October 2026). Other trackers report different totals, so counts are best read as relative.
  • Grid carbon EPA eGRID 2023 CO₂ output emission rate, in pounds per megawatt-hour, for each state.
  • Water stress WRI Aqueduct 4.0 baseline water stress: the share of available surface and groundwater that is withdrawn each year, scored 0 (low) to 5 (extremely high). WRI scores river basins; each state's score is the area-weighted average of the basins inside it, using the U.S. extract shared by the independent analysis.
  • Rainfall NOAA state average annual precipitation, 1971–2000.
  • Hubs Metro-level figures. Capacity from JLL (mid-2025), rainfall at the main airport station (1991–2020), and carbon for the regional grid that serves the metro, so hub and state numbers can differ.
  • Calculator Water and energy follow Li et al. (2023). Method PUE and WUE come from the team's prototype. The team's simplified assumptions: climate shifts air and evaporative efficiency (cooler or more humid air means less evaporation and more free cooling); the fit score starts at 100 and subtracts points for stress-weighted on-site water, power plant water, energy (PUE), racks above the air-cooling limit, and cost or fluid risk; switching costs are $0.1M (air), $0.2M (evaporative), $0.4M (direct-to-chip) and $0.6M (immersion) per MW.
  • Map colors Water stress uses WRI's five categories (Low through Extremely High). The other measures group states into seven equal-count bands.

Limitations

  • Water stress is a state average. A single basin can be far more stressed than its state's score, and a large, lightly used area can pull a state's average down. Hawaii is not covered.
  • Hub numbers mix levels. Hub capacity, rainfall and grid carbon are metro-level, but water stress is the statewide average.
  • Cooling fit ratings are qualitative. They are the team's judgment based on the sources, not measured results.
  • Company disclosure is limited. Most operators do not report which cooling method each site uses.
